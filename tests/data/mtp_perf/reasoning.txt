We want to decide whether speculative decoding can pay for itself on a memory-bound decode
workload. The argument is easiest to make in terms of bytes moved per accepted token. A
target-only decode step reads every weight of the model exactly once and produces one token.
Its cost is therefore dominated by weight traffic divided by achievable bandwidth. A
speculative iteration reads the MTP draft weights K times, reads the target weights once over
K+1 positions, and produces between one and K+1 tokens depending on how many drafts the
target accepts.

Suppose the target weight traffic is T bytes and the MTP draft weight traffic is D bytes per
draft step. If the draft accepts a fraction p of its proposals on average, then a K-draft
iteration produces approximately 1 + p*K tokens and moves T + K*D bytes plus the verification
overhead. The target-only baseline moves T bytes per token. Speculation wins exactly when

    (T + K*D) / (1 + p*K) < T,

which rearranges to

    D < T * p.

In other words, the draft must be strictly cheaper per proposed token than the target times the
acceptance rate. This is a clean necessary condition and it explains why a large draft model is
usually a losing proposition: if D is comparable to T, only a very high acceptance rate can
save you. A single-layer MTP head is attractive precisely because D is small relative to T, but
it is not free: the language-model head alone reads the full embedding matrix, and if that
matrix dominates the draft cost then D is not small at all.

The second observation is that verification is not free either. Even a perfect draft must be
checked, and the check runs the target over K+1 positions. That is more work than a single
target step, though much of it is parallel and therefore bandwidth-limited rather than
latency-limited. The break-even analysis above assumes verification costs the same as a normal
target step per position, which is optimistic in the small-batch regime where fixed overheads
matter. A careful engineer measures the cost side and the acceptance side separately, because a
change that improves one can silently worsen the other, and only the product of the two
determines the outcome.
