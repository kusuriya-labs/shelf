The history of computing is, in large part, a history of abstraction. Each generation of
engineers has discovered that a problem which once seemed irreducible could be decomposed
into smaller pieces, and that those pieces could be recombined in ways the original
designers never anticipated. The stored-program computer replaced the plugboard, the
compiler replaced the hand-written instruction sequence, and the operating system replaced
the careful orchestration of jobs by a human operator. None of these transitions was
instantaneous, and none was uncontested. There were always practitioners who argued that the
new layer of indirection cost more than it saved, that the machine was fast enough as it
was, and that the abstraction would leak in precisely the cases that mattered. They were
sometimes right in the short term and almost always wrong in the long term, because
abstraction lowers the cost of the next experiment.

Consider the memory hierarchy. A processor that can only read from a single flat address
space is simple to reason about, but it cannot sustain the throughput that modern workloads
demand. The cache, the TLB, the prefetcher, and the memory controller each add a layer of
policy that the programmer cannot see and cannot directly control. The result is a system
whose average behavior is excellent and whose worst-case behavior is difficult to predict.
Performance engineering in such a world is less about issuing the theoretically optimal
instruction sequence and more about arranging the work so that the hardware's heuristics
have an easy job. This is why data layout, access order, and working-set size dominate so
many optimization discussions, and why a change that looks trivial in the source can move
throughput by a factor of two.

The same tension appears in machine learning systems. A model is a mathematical object, but
an inference engine is a physical one. The mathematically equivalent formulation that
materializes fewer intermediate tensors, touches memory in a more predictable order, or
keeps the arithmetic units busier will win, often by a wide margin. This is not a failure of
theory; it is a reminder that theory describes the computation while the machine determines
the cost. Good systems work keeps both pictures in view at once and refuses to let either
one become a comfortable fiction.
